Guwahati’s 800-Run ODI: Did the Pitch Serve
Cricket’s Core? 
The Assam Tribune 2026-10-01T12:34:04
The game saw West Indies post 405 for seven, their highest ODI total, before India chased it down with eight wickets in
hand. While the spectacle of 800-plus runs and multiple centuries dominated headlines, critics like India head coach
Gautam Gambhir argued that the pitch failed to create a meaningful contest between bat and ball. "You talk about cricket,
but this is not cricket, this is only batting," Gambhir told Star Sports after the match, emphasizing that a good ODI should
test all three disciplines equally. 
Chief Minister Himanta Biswa Sarma, however, celebrated the pitch’s batting-friendly nature, calling it "an absolute
delight" on social media. His perspective reflects a growing debate: Should a pitch be judged by its ability to produce
high scores, or by its capacity to challenge batters, bowlers, and fielders alike? 
India’s batting coach Sitanshu Kotak added another layer to the discussion, questioning whether modern batting standards
—powered by fitness, technology, and aggressive strokeplay—should influence pitch expectations. He noted that no pitch
can guarantee a specific outcome, but the Guwahati surface’s consistency in producing high totals (including India’s 373
in 2023 and a 322 chase in 2018) raises concerns about its balance. 
The core issue lies in whether the pitch rewarded skill across all aspects of the game or merely amplified batting
dominance. If 400 runs no longer feel challenging and bowlers struggle to find traction, the surface may have lost its
ability to create uncertainty—the very essence of cricket. The Guwahati ODI’s record books may shine, but the debate
over what constitutes a "good" pitch remains unresolved.
